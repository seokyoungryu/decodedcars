Once you have settled on a model, the next decision is which year to buy. It sounds minor, but two cars that look nearly identical on a dealer lot can come from different generations, with different engines, transmissions and electronics. Choosing the year carefully is one of the cheapest ways to lower your risk.

## Learn the model's generation history

Most models are fully redesigned every several years. Each redesign starts a new **generation**: new body, often a new platform, and frequently new powertrains. Between redesigns, the car stays mostly the same from year to year.

Before you shop, find out:

- **Which years belong to each generation.** A quick look at the year pages for a model (for example /toyota/rav4/2019 versus the year before) shows when body style, dimensions or engine options changed.
- **Which engines and transmissions were offered.** A new engine or a new type of transmission is a bigger change than new styling.
- **When the mid-cycle refresh happened.** Many models get a facelift partway through a generation, with updated styling, features and sometimes mechanical fixes.

## Why the first year of a generation carries more risk

The first model year of a new generation is when the factory, suppliers and the engineering team are all doing something new at volume. Problems that did not show up in testing often appear once hundreds of thousands of owners start driving the car in every climate. Manufacturers usually fix those issues in production over the following year or two.

That does not make every first-year car a bad buy. But if two years of the same generation are similar in price and features, the **second or third year** is usually the safer bet. The same logic applies to a brand-new engine or transmission introduced in an otherwise carried-over model.

## Mid-cycle refreshes: upgrade or new risk?

A refresh can go either way:

- If it was mostly cosmetic (new bumpers, lights, a larger screen), reliability usually carries over from the earlier years.
- If it added a new powertrain or a new driver-assistance system, treat that part like a first-year item.
- Refreshes sometimes quietly fix known weak points. When complaints about a particular component drop sharply after the refresh year, that is a good sign.

## Use the problems-by-year page

Every model on DecodedCars has a problems-by-year page, such as /honda/cr-v/problems. It shows recalls and owner complaints for each model year side by side, which is exactly what you need for this decision.

How to read it for a used purchase:

- **Look for spikes, not totals.** One year that stands out from its neighbors in the same generation deserves a closer look. Open that year and check which components dominate.
- **Check the component, not just the count.** A spike in complaints about infotainment is annoying; a spike about the engine, transmission, brakes or steering is more serious and more expensive.
- **Adjust for age.** Older years have had more time to collect reports, and the newest years have had very little. Compare years of similar age when you can. Our [guide to complaint counts](/guide/read-complaints-mpg) explains this in more detail.
- **Read recalls as fixable items.** A recall on the year you want is not automatically a reason to skip it, as long as the repair has been done. See [Is a car with many recalls a bad buy?](/guide/are-recalls-a-red-flag)

## Mileage versus age

Buyers often ask whether a newer car with high miles beats an older car with low miles. There is no single answer, but a few rules hold up well:

- **Highway miles are gentler than city miles.** A car that covered long commutes may have less wear on brakes and transmission than a low-mileage car used for short trips.
- **Age wears out rubber and plastic.** Hoses, belts, seals, tires and batteries degrade with time even when the car sits. A very low-mileage older car can still need these replaced.
- **Typical mileage at failure matters.** On each model-year page, the median mileage owners reported for problems hints at whether a known issue tends to appear early or later. If you are looking at a car just below that mileage, budget for it or confirm it was already addressed.
- **Maintenance records beat the odometer.** A higher-mileage car with documented oil changes and fluid services is often a better buy than a low-mileage one with no history.

## A simple process

1. Pick the model, then list the generations and their years.
2. Prefer later years within a generation, unless a refresh introduced a new powertrain.
3. Open the problems-by-year page and note any years that stand out, and which components drive them.
4. Narrow to two or three target years and compare prices. Our [compare tool](/compare) can line up specs and data for different years.
5. For the specific car you find, run the VIN for open recalls ([how to check recalls](/guide/check-recalls)) and get an independent pre-purchase inspection.

## Don't overthink small differences

If two years have similar complaint patterns and no major powertrain change, the condition and history of the individual car matter far more than the year. Use the year data to avoid known trouble spots, then let the inspection decide.
