There is no single best powertrain. The right choice depends mostly on how far you drive, where you park at night and the climate you live in.

## The four options in plain terms

- **Gas (conventional):** an engine and a fuel tank. Quick to refuel and usually the cheapest to buy.
- **Hybrid (HEV):** a gas engine plus a small battery and electric motor that recover energy when braking. You never plug it in. The gains are largest in stop-and-go driving.
- **Plug-in hybrid (PHEV):** a bigger battery you charge from an outlet, good for a limited electric range, with a gas engine taking over after that. It only pays off if you actually plug it in regularly.
- **Electric (EV):** a battery and motors, no gas engine. Charged at home, at work or at public chargers.

## Start with your driving pattern

Write down a normal week:

- **Daily distance.** If most days are short and predictable, an EV or PHEV can cover them on electricity. Compare your typical day with the EPA electric range shown on each model's year page.
- **Long trips.** How often do you drive several hundred miles in a day? Occasional road trips work in an EV with planning; frequent ones through areas with few fast chargers favor a hybrid or gas car.
- **City versus highway.** Hybrids shine in traffic and on city streets. On steady highway runs, the difference between a hybrid and an efficient gas car shrinks.

## Home charging is the deciding factor

For EVs and plug-in hybrids, the biggest question is whether you can charge where you park overnight.

- **Level 1** uses a regular household outlet. It is slow, but it can be enough for a PHEV or for an EV driver with a short commute.
- **Level 2** uses a 240-volt circuit, like a dryer outlet, and can refill most EVs overnight. It may require an electrician and, if you rent, the landlord's permission.
- **Public fast charging** is convenient on trips but usually costs more per mile than home charging, and relying on it every day erodes the savings.

If you cannot charge at home or work, a regular hybrid is often the more practical way to cut fuel use.

## Do the fuel cost math with your own numbers

EPA's annual fuel cost figure on our pages uses a standard 15,000 miles and national average prices, which makes cars comparable. Your own cost depends on your mileage and your local prices for gas and electricity, which vary a lot by state and by utility plan.

Use the [fuel cost calculator](/tools/fuel-cost) with:

- your real yearly miles,
- your local gas price,
- your electricity rate (check your utility bill; some utilities offer cheaper overnight rates).

Then compare the yearly difference with the difference in purchase price. If the gap in price would take many years to recover, the cheaper car may be the better deal even if it uses more fuel. ## Battery warranty basics

Battery cost is the main worry for many buyers, especially on used hybrids and EVs. Some things to know:

- **Federal rules** require an emissions warranty on certain major emissions components for 8 years or 80,000 miles. Many manufacturers cover the hybrid battery for at least that long, sometimes as part of that warranty, but the details depend on the vehicle and how the part is classified.
- **California and states that follow its rules** require longer coverage, up to 10 years or 150,000 miles, for the battery on certain qualifying hybrids and plug-ins.
- **EV batteries** are typically covered by the manufacturer's own warranty, commonly around 8 years and 100,000 miles, often with a minimum capacity guarantee. Terms vary by brand and model year.

Always read the warranty booklet for the exact car, and for a used car check how much time and mileage remain and whether the coverage transfers to a second owner.

## Climate matters

- **Cold weather** reduces EV range noticeably, because the battery is less efficient and heating the cabin uses energy. A heat pump, available on many newer EVs, helps.
- **Extreme heat** is hard on batteries over the long run. Cars with active battery cooling handle it better.
- Hybrids and gas cars also lose some efficiency in winter, but the effect is smaller.

If you live somewhere with harsh winters, size an EV's range with a healthy margin above your daily needs.

## Resale and long-term ownership

- Gas cars and established hybrids have long, predictable resale histories.
- EV resale has been more volatile, influenced by new-car price cuts, changing incentives and fast-improving technology. Buying used can let someone else absorb the early drop.
- For a used EV, ask for a battery health report if the car can provide one, and check recalls and complaints for the battery and charging system on the model's problems page.

## A quick way to decide

- Short commute, home charging, a second car for long trips: an **EV** is worth a serious look.
- Home charging and a mix of short days and long trips: a **plug-in hybrid** can fit, if you will plug in.
- No home charging, lots of city driving: a **hybrid**.
- Low yearly mileage, lowest upfront cost, frequent long rural trips: an efficient **gas** car still makes sense.
