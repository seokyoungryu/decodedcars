Your first car purchase is mostly a series of small decisions made in the right order. Most first-time buyers who end up unhappy did not pick a bad car — they skipped a step, usually the boring ones: the real budget, the inspection or the paperwork. Work through this list from top to bottom and you will avoid the most common mistakes.

## 1. Set a budget based on the monthly total, not the sticker price

The price of the car is only one line of what you will pay. Before you look at a single listing, estimate the full monthly cost of owning it:

- **Loan payment** — use the [car loan calculator](/tools/car-loan) to see how the rate and term change the payment. A longer loan lowers the monthly number but usually raises the total interest you pay, and it can leave you owing more than the car is worth for years.
- **Insurance** — this is the item that surprises first-time buyers most. Rates depend on your age, driving record, ZIP code and the specific car. Get real quotes for two or three candidate models before you commit; a sporty trim or a car that is expensive to repair can cost noticeably more to insure than a plain version of the same model.
- **Fuel or charging** — the [fuel cost tool](/tools/fuel-cost) turns EPA mileage into a yearly cost for your own driving distance.
- **Maintenance and repairs** — oil changes, tires, brakes and the occasional surprise. Older cars out of warranty need a bigger cushion.
- **Registration, taxes and fees** — these vary a lot by state, so check your state's motor vehicle agency.

Leave room for an emergency fund. A car payment you can only just afford becomes a problem the first time the car needs tires and a battery in the same month.

## 2. Decide between new and used

**New** gets you a full factory warranty, the latest safety equipment and a known history — but it loses value fastest in the first few years. **Used** costs less to buy and often less to insure, but you take on the previous owner's history and more risk of repairs.

For many first-time buyers, a **lightly used car a few years old** is a sensible middle ground: much of the early depreciation is gone, and some factory warranty may still remain. If you go used, our guide to [picking the right used model year](/guide/pick-used-model-year) explains how to spot the better years of a model.

## 3. Pick a short list of two to four models

Start with what you actually need: how many people you carry, where you park, how far you drive and whether you deal with snow. Then narrow down:

- Browse [best cars by class](/best) or use the [car finder](/finder) to filter by budget and type.
- Check each model's **problems-by-year page** (for example, /honda/civic/problems) to see which components owners complain about most, and read our guide on [how to read complaint counts, MPG and crash ratings](/guide/read-complaints-mpg).
- Put your finalists side by side in [compare](/compare).
- Make sure it fits where you live with the [garage fit tool](/tools/garage-fit).

Having more than one candidate matters. It keeps you from falling for one specific car and overpaying for it.

## 4. Test drive with a plan

A quick loop around the block tells you very little. Drive the car on roads you actually use — including the highway — and pay attention to:

- **Seating and visibility** — can you get comfortable, see out the back, and reach every control?
- **Noises and vibrations** — clunks over bumps, grinding when braking, or shaking at speed.
- **Warning lights** — every dashboard light should come on briefly at startup and then go out.
- **Everything electrical** — air conditioning, heat, windows, locks, phone pairing and the backup camera.

Test drive at least two candidates — small differences on paper are often obvious from the driver's seat.

## 5. Get a pre-purchase inspection (used cars)

Before you pay for a used car, have an **independent mechanic** you choose look it over. A pre-purchase inspection can find leaks, worn suspension parts, hidden accident repairs or problems the seller did not mention. If a private seller or dealer refuses to let you do this, treat that as a reason to walk away.

## 6. Check the history and the recalls

- Run a **vehicle history report** to look for reported accidents, title problems (salvage, flood, rebuilt), odometer issues and ownership history. A clean report is helpful but not a guarantee — not every accident gets reported.
- Check the **VIN for open recalls**. Our guide on [checking for open recalls](/guide/check-recalls) explains how, and the [VIN decoder](/tools/vin-decoder) confirms the car's year, engine and build details match the listing.

## 7. Handle the paperwork carefully

- **Read the buyer's order line by line.** Ask what every fee is. Optional add-ons such as extended warranties or protection packages can be declined.
- **Arrange financing before you shop** if possible — a pre-approval from a bank or credit union gives you a rate to compare against the dealer's offer.
- **With a private seller,** make sure the name on the title matches the seller's ID and that there is no lien on it. Follow your state's rules for signing over the title and getting a bill of sale.
- **Have insurance in place** before you drive away; most states require it to register the car.
