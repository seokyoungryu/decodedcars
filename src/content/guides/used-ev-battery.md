On a used electric car, the battery pack is the most expensive component — and on a hybrid, it is one of the few big-ticket parts a gas car does not have. Most batteries age gradually rather than failing suddenly, but you cannot see wear by looking at the car, so ask the right questions and check a few things before you buy.

## How batteries wear

Every lithium-ion battery slowly loses capacity with age and use. In an EV, that shows up as **less range** than the car had when new. In a hybrid, a weaker battery usually means the gas engine runs more often, which can lower fuel economy before any warning light appears.

Several things tend to speed up wear:

- **Heat** — cars kept for years in very hot climates, especially without battery cooling, may lose capacity faster.
- **Frequent DC fast charging**, particularly in hot weather.
- **Regularly charging to 100% and leaving the car sitting full**, or leaving it sitting nearly empty for long periods.
- **Age**, even with low mileage — a battery ages with time as well as use.

Battery chemistry, cooling and software differ widely between models, so these factors matter more for some cars than others.

## Ask for a state-of-health reading

**State of health (SOH)** is the battery's current usable capacity compared with when it was new, usually shown as a percentage. It is the most useful single number when buying a used EV.

Ways to get it:

- **A dealer battery report.** Many brand dealers can run a battery health check with their diagnostic tools. Ask for a printed or emailed report.
- **The car's own display.** Some models show battery health or capacity bars in a service or settings menu.
- **An OBD-II reader and app.** For many models, an inexpensive Bluetooth OBD-II adapter paired with a model-specific app can read battery data. Results vary by app and car, so treat them as a guide.
- **A third-party battery test.** Some inspection services and independent EV shops offer battery health testing.

For a hybrid, ask the mechanic doing your pre-purchase inspection to check the hybrid system for stored fault codes and, if they can, the health of the battery modules.

## Do your own range check

Even without a formal report, you can learn a lot from the car itself:

- **Note the estimated range at a known charge level.** Compare it with the model's EPA range, which you can find on its year page on DecodedCars. The car's estimate is affected by recent driving and temperature, so expect it to be lower in cold weather or after highway driving.
- **Watch how the charge drops on your test drive.** A battery that loses charge much faster than the miles driven deserves a closer look.
- **Try charging it**, if the seller allows, to confirm the charging port and onboard charger work on both Level 2 and, if equipped, DC fast charging.

## Look at the charging history

Some cars record how often they were fast-charged, and some brands can show charging data through their apps or dealer tools. Ask the seller:

- How did you usually charge — at home, at work or mostly at fast chargers?
- What charge limit did you normally set?
- Where was the car kept — a garage, or outside in heat or cold?

There are no perfect answers, but clear, consistent ones suggest an owner who paid attention.

## Battery warranty: check what is left

Batteries on new hybrids and EVs sold in the U.S. come with long warranties, and they often transfer to the next owner.

- **Federal emissions warranty.** Under federal rules, hybrid and EV battery components are generally covered as emissions-related parts for **8 years or 80,000 miles**, whichever comes first.
- **California and other states.** Some states that follow California's emissions rules may require longer coverage on certain hybrids — in some cases **10 years or 150,000 miles**.
- **Manufacturer warranties** often go beyond the minimum, and many EV warranties also promise that capacity will not fall below a certain level during the coverage period.

Coverage depends on the specific car, its original sale state and its in-service date — the day it was first sold — not the model year. **Always check the warranty booklet for that exact car**, and ask a brand dealer to confirm what remains using the VIN. Also ask whether the battery has ever been replaced or repaired under warranty.

## Check for battery recalls

Battery packs and high-voltage components do get recalled, sometimes with advice to limit charging or park outside. Before you buy:

- Look up the car's VIN for open recalls — our guide on [checking for open recalls](/guide/check-recalls) explains how.
- Read the model's problems-by-year page (for example, [Chevrolet Bolt EV problems by year](/chevrolet/bolt-ev/problems)) and check which components owners report most.
- If a recall involved a battery replacement or software update, ask for proof it was completed.

## Questions to ask the seller

- Do you have a battery health report, or will you let me get one?
- Has the battery or any high-voltage part ever been replaced or repaired?
- Have all recalls and software updates been done?
- How was the car usually charged, and where was it parked?
- Are the charging cable and any adapters included?
- Do you have service records?

## Putting it together

A healthy battery, remaining warranty and completed recalls make a used EV or hybrid a solid buy.
