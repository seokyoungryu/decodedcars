Many buyers start with a body style and only later discover that the car they chose is bigger, or smaller, than their life requires. Spec sheets can answer most of these questions before you ever visit a dealer, if you know which numbers matter.

## Start with how you use the car

Before comparing body styles, list what the car has to do on a normal week and on the hardest days of the year:

- How many people ride along regularly, and how tall are they?
- Do you need child seats, and how many?
- What is the bulkiest thing you carry: strollers, sports gear, a dog crate, tools?
- Where do you park at home and at work?
- Do you tow, or drive on unpaved roads or in deep snow?

Be honest about the difference between "every week" and "twice a year." Renting or borrowing a larger vehicle for the rare trip can be cheaper than driving a larger vehicle every day.

## How to read the dimensions

DecodedCars shows key dimensions on each model-year page. Here is what each one tells you:

- **Length** decides whether the car fits your garage or parking space and how easy it is to parallel park.
- **Width** affects how much room you have to open doors in a tight garage and how the car feels on narrow streets. Check whether the figure includes the mirrors; many published widths do not.
- **Height** matters for low garage doors, parking structures and roof racks.
- **Wheelbase** is the distance between the front and rear axles. A longer wheelbase usually means more rear legroom and a smoother ride. It is often a better clue to rear-seat space than overall length.
- **Curb weight** is the weight of the car with fluids but no passengers. Heavier cars tend to use more energy, need more braking and wear tires faster, though weight also comes from batteries and safety equipment.

## Sedan, hatchback or SUV

**Sedans** have a separate, enclosed trunk. They usually sit lower, which helps handling and highway efficiency, and the trunk keeps luggage out of sight. The trade-off is a smaller opening, so tall or bulky items are hard to load.

**Hatchbacks** have a rear door that lifts with the glass. On a similar footprint to a small sedan, they offer a much bigger opening and, with the rear seats folded, far more usable cargo space. They are often the most practical choice for city drivers.

**SUVs and crossovers** sit higher and usually have a tall, boxy cargo area. The upright seating is easier to get in and out of, and visibility over traffic is better. Many offer all-wheel drive. In exchange, they typically weigh more and present a larger front area to the wind, which costs some fuel economy compared with a similar sedan or hatchback.

A compact SUV and a midsize sedan can have similar interior space. The difference is the shape of that space: tall and vertical in the SUV, long and low in the sedan.

## Cargo versus passenger space

Interior volume is split between people and luggage, and manufacturers can shift it either way.

- **Cargo volume** is usually listed twice: behind the rear seats and with them folded. The first number matters for daily life with passengers; the second for moving or big shopping trips.
- **Third-row seats** in midsize SUVs often leave little cargo room when in use and can be tight for adults. If you need three rows only occasionally, check how much space remains behind the third row.
- **Passenger volume** and rear legroom figures help compare back-seat comfort, but sit in the car yourself, behind the driver's seat set for your own height.

## Garage fit

A car that technically fits a garage can still be miserable to use if you cannot open the doors or walk around it. Measure your garage length, width and door height, and leave space for opening doors and getting out with bags or a child seat.

The [garage fit tool](/tools/garage-fit) compares your measurements with a car's dimensions so you can see the clearance before you buy.

## Handling and efficiency trade-offs

- **Lower and lighter** generally means more stable cornering, shorter stopping distances and better fuel economy.
- **Taller vehicles** have a higher center of gravity. Modern stability control helps a great deal, but rollover resistance ratings still tend to favor lower vehicles. NHTSA's rollover figures are explained in our [guide to crash ratings](/guide/read-complaints-mpg).
- **All-wheel drive** helps with traction when accelerating in rain or snow, but does not help you stop or steer on ice. Good tires matter more than drivetrain for winter safety.
- **Bigger wheels** look sporty but can make the ride firmer and tires more expensive.

## Narrow it down

1. Write down the minimum seats and cargo you truly need.
2. Measure your parking space.
3. Use the [car finder](/finder) to filter by class, then the [best cars by class](/best) to see well-rated options in that size.
4. Compare two or three finalists on dimensions, MPG and safety with the [compare tool](/compare).
5. Bring the stroller, the car seat or the golf bag to the test drive and try it.

The right size is the smallest car that handles your real routine comfortably. Anything beyond that costs you every day in fuel, parking and handling.
