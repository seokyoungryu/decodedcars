The EPA rates the fuel economy of every new car sold in the U.S., and for electric cars it also rates driving range. We took the best-rated version of each model, year by year, for all the models on DecodedCars and looked at how the numbers moved over two decades. Two very different stories come out.

## Electric range roughly quadrupled

| Model year | EV models rated | Typical (median) range | Longest range |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2014 | 12 | 84 miles | 265 miles |
| 2016 | 13 | 84 miles | 315 miles |
| 2018 | 14 | 125 miles | 335 miles |
| 2019 | 16 | 226 miles | 370 miles |
| 2020 | 16 | 239 miles | 402 miles |
| 2022 | 32 | 270 miles | 520 miles |
| 2024 | 59 | 303 miles | 516 miles |
| 2026 | 66 | 308 miles | 512 miles |

**The jump came around 2019.** Through the mid-2010s, the typical electric car was a small city car with less than 100 miles of rated range — enough for commuting, not for road trips. Between the 2018 and 2019 model years the median nearly doubled, as long-range models replaced the first-generation compacts.

**Then the number of choices exploded.** In 2020 there were 16 electric models on our list; by 2025 there were 72. Many of the newcomers were SUVs and trucks, which carry bigger batteries but also weigh more, so the typical range has grown more slowly since 2022 — from about 270 to about 310 miles.

**The longest range has plateaued.** The record went from 265 miles in 2012 to just over 500 miles in 2022 and has stayed there. Makers have since focused on faster charging and lower prices rather than ever-bigger batteries.

## Gas-car mileage improved, then leveled off

For gas, diesel and hybrid models we looked at the best combined MPG of each model:

| Model year | Typical (median) best MPG | Models at 40+ MPG |
|---|---|---|
| 2006 | 19 | 3 |
| 2010 | 21 | 3 |
| 2014 | 23 | 12 |
| 2018 | 25 | 15 |
| 2022 | 25 | 14 |
| 2026 | 24 | 16 |

**Most of the gain came before 2018.** Turbocharged smaller engines, transmissions with more gears and lighter construction lifted the typical model from 19 to 25 MPG in about twelve years — roughly a 30% improvement, which cuts fuel spending by about a quarter for the same driving.

**Since then, the typical number has stalled.** One reason is the shift in what people buy: SUVs and pickups have taken a larger share of the lineup, and many small, efficient cars were discontinued. The efficiency gains are now concentrated in hybrids — the number of models rated at 40 MPG or better has grown from 3 to 16.

## What this means if you are shopping

- **An older used EV may have far less range than a new one.** A 2016 electric car with 84 miles when new may have less today after battery wear. Check the EPA range on the model-year page and our guide to [checking a used EV battery](/guide/used-ev-battery).
- **Don't overpay for a range you won't use.** If you drive 40 miles a day and can charge at home, a 250-mile EV covers you with room to spare. See [hybrid, EV or gas](/guide/hybrid-ev-or-gas).
- **A newer gas car isn't automatically more efficient.** Within a class, the difference between a 2018 and a 2024 model is often small. Compare the exact years in the [fuel cost calculator](/tools/fuel-cost).
- **Hybrids are where the big MPG gains are.** If you want low fuel costs without plugging in, compare hybrid versions — the price gap over the gas version is often smaller than people expect.

*Source: EPA fuel economy ratings (fueleconomy.gov) for the models on DecodedCars, best-rated version of each model per model year. Medians count each model once; model years before 2014 had too few electric models to compare. Range figures are EPA ratings when new, not real-world results.*
