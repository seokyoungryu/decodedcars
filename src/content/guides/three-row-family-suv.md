Plenty of vehicles advertise seven or eight seats. Far fewer make all of them easy to use every day with real children, car seats and a week of groceries. The spec sheet tells you a vehicle has a third row; only a careful look tells you whether your family will use it.

If you are still deciding whether you need this much vehicle at all, start with our guide on [SUV vs sedan size](/guide/suv-vs-sedan-size).

## Who will sit in the third row?

Be honest about how you will use the back row, because the answer changes which vehicles make sense:

- **Occasionally, for short trips** — many midsize three-row SUVs are fine. Their third rows are often best for children.
- **Every day, for school runs or carpools** — access and comfort matter far more. Larger SUVs and minivans usually do better here.
- **Adults on longer drives** — look at the biggest SUVs and minivans.

## Test third-row access in person

How you get into the third row matters as much as how roomy it is. At the dealer, try it the way your family will:

- **Slide or tilt the second row.** Some second-row seats tilt forward with a child seat still installed, which saves you from removing it every time someone climbs in back. Check whether your specific vehicle and seat allow this.
- **Captain's chairs vs a bench.** Two second-row captain's chairs leave a walkway to the third row but drop one seat. A bench seats one more person but usually means folding a seat to reach the back.
- **Watch the step-in height and door opening.** Smaller children need to climb in on their own; a high floor and a narrow gap make that harder.
- **Sit back there.** Check knee room, foot room under the seat ahead, headroom and whether there are air vents, cupholders and charging ports.

## Car seats and LATCH

If you have young children, car seats will shape the decision more than anything else.

- **Count the LATCH positions.** LATCH (Lower Anchors and Tethers for Children) uses built-in anchors to secure a car seat. Not every seating position has lower anchors, and some third rows have only one or none. The owner's manual shows exactly which seats do.
- **Top tethers matter too.** Forward-facing seats should use the top tether. Find out where the tether anchors are for each row.
- **Bring your own car seats** to the test drive. Install them, especially a rear-facing infant seat, and check that the front passenger still has enough legroom.
- **Three across.** If you need three car seats in one row, measure carefully — this depends on the width of both the vehicle and the specific seats.

A certified child passenger safety technician can check your installation for free in many communities.

## Cargo space behind the third row

This is where many three-row SUVs disappoint. With every seat in use, the space behind the third row can be small — sometimes barely enough for a stroller. Published cargo figures are measured in different ways, so the best test is physical:

- Bring your **stroller, sports bags or a cooler** and see if they fit with the third row up.
- Check for a **deep well behind the third row** — minivans and some SUVs have one, which adds useful height.
- See how easy the **third row is to fold** and whether the second row folds flat as well.

If you often need all seats *and* lots of cargo space, that alone can point you toward a minivan or one of the largest SUVs.

## Minivan vs three-row SUV

Neither is better for everyone. Here is how they usually compare:

**Minivans tend to offer:**

- **Sliding doors** — easy entry in tight parking spots, with no worry about kids swinging a door into the next car.
- **A low floor**, which makes climbing in and loading easier.
- **More usable third-row and cargo space** for their size.

**Three-row SUVs tend to offer:**

- **More ground clearance** for rough roads and deep snow.
- **Higher towing capacity** in many models, if you pull a trailer or a boat.
- **Wider availability of AWD or 4WD** — though some minivans offer AWD too. See [AWD vs 4WD vs FWD vs RWD explained](/guide/awd-vs-4wd).

Fuel economy varies more by model and powertrain than by body type, and hybrid versions of both exist. Check each version's EPA figures on its year page and use the [fuel cost tool](/tools/fuel-cost) to compare.

## Safety features to look for

Bigger does not automatically mean safer, so look at the specifics:

- **Crash ratings** — check each model year's NHTSA star ratings on its year page. Our guide on [reading crash ratings](/guide/read-complaints-mpg) explains what they mean.
- **Automatic emergency braking** with pedestrian detection.
- **Blind-spot monitoring and rear cross-traffic alert** — very helpful in a long vehicle when backing out of a parking space.
- **Rear-seat reminder**, which alerts the driver to check the back seat when parking.
- **Child locks and window lockouts** that the driver can control.
- **A clear backup camera**, ideally with a surround view on larger vehicles.

Also look up open recalls and the model's problems-by-year page (for example, [Honda Odyssey problems by year](/honda/odyssey/problems)) — family vehicles often see heavy use, and you want to know about common issues before you buy.
